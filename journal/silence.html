What Silence Sounds Like in Paint

April 2026 · Berlin

This essay pushes back on the idea that a quiet painting is simply a restful one. For OBOSHI, silence isn't a mood — it's a material: the negative space between marks, the moment an arm is moving but hasn't yet landed. The comparison is acoustic — a note played in an empty room reads differently than the same note in a full one, because the silence around it changes what it is.

He describes building this into his layered process: a ground, then color, then marks, then material like washi paper, tape, or prints of earlier paintings, then areas left deliberately untouched or thinned back down toward the canvas. Each new layer changes how the silence beneath it reads — the first mark he ever makes on a canvas is the quietest, because it has nothing yet to respond to.

Washi paper gets particular attention here for the way it scatters rather than reflects light, giving a surface that breathes unevenly rather than sitting flat. When it carries printed fragments of his own earlier paintings, the effect isn't self-reference so much as time made material — a history embedded in the work that can be felt without being consciously seen.

The essay's sharper claim is that resonance requires tension, not calm — a tuning fork vibrates because it's been struck, not because it's relaxed. What a viewer ultimately feels in front of the work, he argues, isn't a message the painting delivers but a resonance that depends on what they themselves bring to it.
