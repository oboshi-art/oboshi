Essays from the Studio

Each Time I Play, It Slips.

May 2026 · Berlin

OBOSHI opens with the idea that a painting doesn't always begin from clarity — sometimes it begins from a small hesitation, a slight tilt in the hand before meaning has had time to form, and that tilt becomes the work's actual starting point. The desire to layer slip upon slip, in his account, isn't a wish to destroy meaning so much as a way of positioning the work just outside it, in a quiet zone where nothing has fixed yet.

He frames the tension here as one between skill and essence: growing skilled at organizing a composition is a sign of real progress, but that same skill can quietly smooth over the rawer sense that first guided the hand, making it easier to drift away from the very place the work is meant to stand. Returning to that place doesn't require giving up skill — only shifting it slightly sideways so the work can breathe again.

A short dialogue with Copilot plays out this tension directly: the wish to make something incomprehensible keeps running into an instinct to organize it, and the exchange lands on the idea that the way forward isn't to reverse course back toward the incomprehensible, but to keep letting slips accumulate. The essay closes on the idea that a slip doesn't blur a work so much as guide it back to where it belongs — the tension between meaning-making and letting go becoming, in itself, the work's breath.
